Two things go in: the card statement (the charges) and the receipts (the proof). One statement can hold several cards at once. Receipts can come however they already exist: exported from Zoho Expense (the usual way), typed into a simple file, or a folder of photos and PDFs that the tool reads on its own.

How Chris uses it

She opens a private web link and signs in with a password, so only Brisken can reach it. She uploads the month's statement and receipts and starts it; it runs on its own while she does something else.

When it finishes she gets a screen: every charge next to the receipt the tool paired it with, and how sure it is. She confirms the good ones, fixes the rest, and for anything left over she picks the receipt herself. A bar at the bottom shows when the month is fully checked, and only then can she download the results, so a half-finished month can't slip out by accident.

Pairing charges with receipts

The tool only calls the AI when a plain match won't settle it. Same card, same day, same amount: paired on the spot. Travel is where it earns its keep.

A receipt in euros sits against a charge that posted in dollars, so they never match to the cent. The tool measures the gap once converted and sorts each pair into three bands:

The middle band is wide on purpose. Cards often add their own markup when they convert at the till, up to about 13% on Brisken's own receipts, so a charge can run high and still be the right receipt. When the receipt prints a dollar figure, the tool trusts that over any estimate. The bands came from real Brisken receipts, not a guess.

Banks also chop merchant names: Chase prints MEGA CENTE CONSTR for a shop with a much longer name. The tool still lines the chopped name up with the full one on the receipt. The name is a tie-breaker, never the whole basis for a match.

Whatever happens, no charge disappears. Every one lands in exactly one of three places, and you can see the split at a glance:

Matched
Paired with its receipt, ready for the journal.
Needs a look
A currency gap, or two receipts that both fit. Set aside for Chris with the tool's reasoning shown.
Unmatched
No receipt, or no charge. Flagged so the gap is visible, never hidden.

Sorting each charge to an account

The tool reads what's on the receipt and files each line under one of eight everyday categories: travel, meals, software, office supplies, equipment, marketing, professional services, and premises. It goes by what was bought, not where, so a drill from a supermarket still counts as equipment. When it isn't sure, it leaves the account blank for Chris rather than guess, and it won't use an account that isn't in Brisken's books. The AI does this sorting when it's switched on; switched off, a simpler word-match does it and the run still finishes.

It learns each month

When Chris is happy with a month, one button teaches the tool what she decided: this merchant is that category, this short bank name is that shop, this is the going exchange rate. Next month those arrive already filled in, so each run is quicker than the last. A separate page shows everything it has learned, and she can make it forget anything that turned out wrong.

What you get back

Two files, and only once every charge is checked. A review copy in Excel (a summary, one tab per card, and tabs for anything that needs a look), and the journal to import into Zoho Books. Every journal line carries a link to its receipt and the report it came from, so the proof is one click away.

Staying in control

  • Nothing is final until every charge is checked and the month is downloaded.
  • Nothing reaches Zoho automatically; the import is always a person.
  • The site is password-protected and runs on EU servers; the password can change at any time.
  • The tool keeps its own copy of the statements and receipts, so the history doesn't depend on Zoho staying switched on.
