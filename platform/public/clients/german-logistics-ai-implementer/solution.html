Capabilities

What I bring to the implementation seat

The product is yours. My job is to be the person who makes it land cleanly inside each new logistics tenant, in their language, on their stack.

Python and FastAPI

I'd be productive in your codebase by tenant 2. I read service boundaries quickly: where the API layer ends, where the worker tasks pick up, where tenant-scoped config branches. ARQ on Redis is a known pattern for me (job queue with retries and scheduled work), so no surprises there. AWS Bedrock with Claude is the API I already use professionally, so per-tenant model selection and prompt versioning are config touches, not architecture decisions.

The tenant 1 onboarding is where the learning curve sits. By tenant 2 the unknowns are tenant-specific (their tone, their terminology, their escalation rules), not stack-specific.

Multi-tenant SaaS configuration

Tenant setup in practice means a fairly long checklist: branding (logos, accent colors, sender display name), module flags (which workflows are enabled for this tenant), IMAP and SMTP credentials, per-tenant business rules, and the human escalation contacts. UnpauseAI's pattern is to enumerate every config touchpoint up front before touching anything, so nothing gets missed at handoff.

The risk in multi-tenant config is the silent skip: a flag left at the default, a credential set against the wrong tenant ID, a template that still has the previous tenant's voice. The fix is a kickoff intake form that mirrors the config surface 1:1 and a smoke test before the onboarding call, not after.

Prompt and workflow customization

Adapting prompts, templates, and rules to fit a specific logistics company means a few things, in order:

  • Tone. Formal Sie by default, but some tenants run a friendlier register with their B2B customers. That's a template choice, not a prompt choice.
  • Terminology. Sendung vs. Lieferung, Lieferschein vs. Frachtbrief, Paket vs. Sendung. Each tenant has its own house vocabulary; the templates pick that up.
  • Escalation rules. Where model judgment ends and a human takes over is a per-tenant design decision: address corrections, refund mentions, complaint language. I make those boundaries explicit per tenant, not implicit.

Where the rules end and the model's judgment begins is the design call. I document it per tenant so the post-30-day quality check is comparing actuals against something written down, not against a vibe.

German customer calls

The onboarding call format I'd run, by default:

  • 60 to 90 minutes, in German, recorded on request.
  • Walkthrough of the configured tenant: branding live, module flags visible, credentials wired.
  • IMAP and SMTP live test with one real test ticket end to end.
  • Q&A, then a written summary in German sent within 24 hours.

The format flexes per tenant: some teams want a longer Q&A, some want a shorter walkthrough and more dry-run tickets. The call is the moment the tenant decides whether they trust the system, so I treat it as the actual deliverable, not paperwork around the deliverable.

First weeks of support

"First weeks" in practice:

  • A Slack or email channel I'm on, responsive within the same business day for the first 2 weeks.
  • A daily check of the tenant's error log for the first 2 weeks. Tune anything that surfaces.
  • A weekly summary email: what triggered, what was auto-handled, what needed human attention, what got tuned and why.
  • A 30-day quality check on the calendar, in writing, with the bonus criteria attached.

This is the part that turns "the tenant went live" into "the tenant stays live". It's also the part that surfaces whatever the kickoff intake missed.

Where I stay out

I deliberately don't try to be the architect. The stack is yours. I'm the person who takes it from sold to live for tenant N, then for tenant N+1.
