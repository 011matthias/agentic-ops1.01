How I would ship the 2,000 product matching MVP

Step 1, Ingest

Every supplier CSV goes through one normalize function. Lowercase, strip whitespace, parse numeric specs out of free-text fields, generate a deterministic content hash per row. Keep provenance (which supplier, which file, which date) on every row so we can trace anything later.

Output: one clean SQLite or parquet file, one row per supplier-product pair, with normalized fields plus provenance.

Step 2, Dedupe and flag

Fuzzy match on three signals together: normalized SKU, brand, and a spec hash. Two products match if they agree on brand and at least two of {SKU prefix, spec hash, exact title}. Matched rows are merged with both supplier provenance kept.

Rows missing critical specs go into a triage queue rather than getting dropped. The triage queue is just a flagged subset the team can review and either fill in or accept as-is. Dropping data silently is the wrong default for a tool the team has to trust.

Step 3, Embed

Each canonical product is embedded with OpenAI text-embedding-3-small on a composed string: title plus brand plus normalized specs. Vectors stored locally in FAISS or sqlite-vec (whichever fits the deploy target). Re-embed only on row change. Cache aggressively.

Cost note: text-embedding-3-small is cheap (fractions of a cent per row at this volume). The cost concern is on search-time LLM calls, not on bulk embedding.

Step 4, Search with confidence

Query goes through the same embed function, top-K by cosine similarity, results return with a visible confidence number (cosine score rescaled to 0 to 1). That score is the part most search tools skip. It is what tells the team "trust this one" versus "double-check that one" without having to guess from the result list.

Before the similarity ranking, apply a hard categorical filter: brand and category must match (when known) before we even score. This is where the deterministic layer earns its rent. Embeddings will happily tell you that "Phillips screwdriver" and "Philips electronics" are similar; rules say they are not.

Step 5, UI and feedback loop

Thin Next.js or Streamlit interface. Search box, results grid with confidence scores, compare view side-by-side, "this match is wrong" button. The wrong-button writes to a corrections table I can use later to tune ranking weights or fine-tune a re-ranker.

The feedback loop is the thing that makes this work over time. The initial model is fine; what makes it good in week 4 is the team's actual queries and corrections.

Edge cases I would design around from day one

Phillips vs Philips

Embeddings confuse semantically-near terms that are different products. Categorical filter (brand + category) goes before similarity ranking. Cheaper, faster, fewer surprises.

Same product, slightly different specs across suppliers

Common reality. My approach: keep both rows, flag as candidate-duplicates with a confidence score, let the team confirm or split rather than auto-merge. Wrong auto-merges are harder to undo than missed merges.

Missing specs are the norm, not the exception

Triage queue with flagging rather than silent drops. The team gets visibility into what's missing. Optional next step: a small focused LLM call to fill obvious specs from the product description text, with confidence on each filled field.

Buy vs build, the honest answer

The job post is explicit about this: if a cheap SaaS solves it better, say so. Three real options on the table for product matching at your scale:

  • Algolia with their JS SDK, custom ranking, faceted filters. Strong if your data is mostly clean and the team is comfortable in their dashboard.
  • Pinecone or Weaviate as a hosted vector DB plus your own thin app. Lower per-feature flexibility than custom, more than Algolia. Good middle.
  • Custom (this proposal). Best when the data is messy enough that the cleanup and confidence-scoring matter as much as the search, or when you want full ownership of the matching logic and corrections loop.

The fastest way to answer "which one?" is the paid test. Real data, two suppliers, 200 to 500 products. The matching quality on your actual data tells us more than any whiteboard discussion.
